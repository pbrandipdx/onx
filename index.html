Content landscape and strategy

Part oneThe landscape

Where Offroad's content is sourced, how it travels from third-party and agency data to a phone with no signal, how long each piece of a trail record stays true, and how the same problem looks from outside Offroad.

02

How it gets from source to screen

Six stages, each with its own delay. By the time a claim reaches somebody at a trailhead it has been through all of them — and the last one happens on the customer's device, days or weeks before they arrive.

STAGE 01

Agencies publish

Each source moves on its own calendar. MVUMs are republished annually, per forest, as geospatial PDFs. County assessors update parcels whenever they update them. Imagery lands on a vendor's release schedule.

DelaySet entirely outside onX. Nothing here is negotiable on cadence — only on access.
STAGE 02

onX reconciles

The in-house geospatial team cross-references overlapping sources rather than trusting a single one, cleans geometry, and resolves conflicts through a mixture of manual and automatic review. As of 2023 that was 1.6 million edits to proprietary map content.

NoteThis is the stage onX controls, and the stage where a human last confirmed anything.
STAGE 03

Trail Guides enrich

Agencies supply geometry and legality. Contributors supply what an agency never records — obstacle size, water crossings, cell service, where it's worth stopping. The gap between "a road exists" and "here's what it's like" is filled by hand, one trail at a time — and it's the hardest content in the product to produce.

NoteEnrichment happens at submission. Whether anything schedules a return visit is one of the first things I'd want to understand.
STAGE 04

onX publishes

Verified changes flow into the next app update. onX states that private and government land data updates every one to two years, and that satellite imagery averages four years old.

DelayBatched. A correction verified today waits for the next area update.
STAGE 05

The customer downloads

Offline Maps include all visible features from the selected area — trails, land boundaries, the lot. Which means the record effectively freezes twice: once when onX publishes, and again when the customer saves it to their phone. That isn't a flaw — offline-first is the correct architecture for this category. It does mean freshness has a second, longer clock nobody controls.

DelayUnbounded. A map downloaded before a trip is a snapshot of a snapshot.
STAGE 06

Errors route back

Feedback goes to the mapping team, gets verified against sources, and is corrected on the next area update. onX says plainly that they use these error reports to help decide where to focus their efforts.

NoteA prioritisation loop already exists and works. The question is whether it could be fed by risk as well as by complaints.

What's already working

Worth saying plainly, because it's the foundation everything below depends on — and because several of these are things competitors can't replicate.

  • Built rather than licensed. Most competitors resell a vendor's network. onX conflates thousands of sources and resolves the conflicts in-house — 1.6M+ manual edits as of 2023.
  • Conflict resolution as a discipline. Cross-referencing multiple agency sources instead of trusting any single one is slower, more expensive, and more accurate.
  • Unusual public transparency. Very few mapping companies publish their own refresh cadences. onX states imagery averages four years and land data updates every one to two.
  • Real-time pipelines already exist. NIFC wildfire, precipitation radar, temperature, air quality — the infrastructure for currency is built and shipping.
  • Trail Reports are well designed. Date, vehicle type, status and a real name. Structured, attributed, and richer than passive telemetry could produce.
  • A prioritisation loop exists. Error reports route to the mapping team and already help decide where to focus effort.
  • The contributor program is real. Paid Trail Guides, a defined submission bar, and a Contributor Portal — a durable moat, not a marketing program.
  • Offline-first is the right architecture. For a category with no signal, the constraint is correctly chosen even though it makes freshness harder.

What this adds up to

Reading the pipeline above as a business rather than a system. These are the pressures I'd expect Offroad's content roadmap to be sitting under — and where I'd expect each one to push. I'd want every line of this corrected by someone who can see the internals.

Which means the content roadmap and the pricing model are the same object. Two asymmetries worth asking about. How discounting actually works

The rules are more revealing than the numbers, and onX documents them plainly.

  • Seasonal sales are deepBlack Friday has run up to 40% off new Elite and 30% off new Premium.
  • Codes work only on onxmaps.comNot the App Store or Google Play — discounts steer buyers to the channel onX keeps the margin on.
  • New purchases onlyOne-time use, and only by new or Basic users. Codes can't be applied to an active membership.
  • Renewals are excludedonX's own support page tells existing members to cancel, let the membership expire, then apply the code when resubscribing.
  • Standing segment discountsMilitary, government, first responder and student, alongside a 7-day free trial on every app.

Two things follow. Discounting is a channel strategy as much as a price strategy — every code routes a purchase through onX's own checkout rather than an app store, which is the same reason the open Growth PM role names web checkout explicitly.

And every discount points at acquisition. The deepest cut is on Elite — the tier unlocked by the most expensive licensed content onX carries. Renewal gets nothing, to the point that the documented way to use a code is to cancel and come back. For a seasonal annual subscription, renewal is precisely where content quality should show up: someone renews because last season's map held up. So content's clearest commercial job is retention, while the pricing machinery is aimed at acquisition. Which of those content is currently measured against is a question I'd want to ask early.

How the competition stays current

Now outward. Four apps that face versions of the same problem — a network that changes faster than anyone can survey it — under very different constraints. Waze solves live conditions. Google Maps solves whether the base map is still true, which is nearer to onX's problem. AllTrails and Gaia are the two onX is actually measured against. Most of these answers depend on assumptions off-road doesn't get. A few survive.

The transferable idea isn't gamification — onX's compensated model buys depth that points can't. It's the prompt. Google asks a specific question of someone who is demonstrably in the right place: is this still true? onX knows when a customer has just driven a mapped trail, in a known vehicle, on a known date. That's a better claim to the same question than proximity is.

And the agency path is worth noticing. Google gives land managers a way to correct the map directly. A ranger district that spots a wrong designation in onX today has no obvious route in — which is a relationship asset going unused, in a role whose whole job is agency relationships.

AllTrails and Gaia — the same problem, different bets

The two apps onX is compared to most often. One bets on scale, the other on layers, and neither resolves currency for the customer.

AllTrails

Scale as the answer
Content model
Roughly 450,000 curated trails and 55 million registered users. Review-driven, photo-heavy, community-first.
How conditions stay current
Trail condition reports sit in the free tier — reviews and photos double as the condition signal. Volume does the work.
Known failure mode
Reviewers report routes running through closed areas, private property and active forestry. The advice given to users is to scroll to the most recent reviews rather than the top-rated ones, and to distrust a trail with only one upload and no corroboration.

Gaia GPS

Layers as the answer
Content model
Depth of map layers over curated description — a very large layer library, with community photos and reviews attached to trail search.
How conditions stay current
Each layer refreshes on its own source's cadence. Trail-level description is comparatively thin; the user is expected to read terrain themselves.
Known failure mode
Little to interpret the terrain for you. Strong for a capable navigator, weaker for someone deciding whether a route suits their vehicle or ability.

onX

Curation as the answer
Content model
Compensated Trail Guides producing deep, attributed records over a 650K+ mile network reconciled in-house.
How conditions stay current
Trail Reports — structured, dated, attributed, and gated to Premium or Elite.
Known failure mode
Independent reviewers note that off the beaten path, trails are likely to have zero user submissions — which matches what I found opening a real Guided Trail in the Gifford Pinchot.

AllTrails' weakness is the instructive one. At their scale there are enough reports that recency itself becomes the signal — which is why the standard advice is to read the newest reviews rather than the best ones. That's a customer performing confidence scoring by hand because the product won't do it for them.

onX has the opposite shape: fewer contributions, but far higher-trust ones — named, structured, vehicle-specific. That's a real advantage, and it comes with a constraint. onX can't rely on volume to wash out staleness the way AllTrails can. With sparse contributions, the product has to say how current something is, because the customer has no second report to compare against.

The offline constraint is genuine and onX's architecture answers it correctly, which is why Waze's playbook can't simply be run here. But onX already records tracks. Go & Track captures a route, and onX's own documentation says tracks sync when the device returns to coverage. A recorded track along a trail is direct evidence that a vehicle drove it, on a date, in a known vehicle class — a passive verification signal that already exists and isn't being used as one. That's the piece of the Waze model that survives the offline constraint: not real-time, but eventually-consistent proof of passage.

The cheap version of the confirmation loop survives too. Waze asks the next driver whether the hazard is still there. onX could ask one question when a track ends on a mapped trail: did it match the description? One tap, at the moment the memory is freshest, from someone who just proved they were there.

Where I'd focus first

Five steps. The first three are schema and pipeline work. The last two are what turn a data property into a program the Trail Guide team can run against.

STEP 01

Classify

Tag every field with its provenance and its expected decay rate. Not every record — every field. A record is a blend, so a single freshness value for the whole thing is a lie.

Output content class registry
STEP 02

Stamp

Carry last_verified and verified_by at field level through the pipeline. Today a Trail Report has a date. An authored difficulty rating does not.

Output schema change
STEP 03

Score

Confidence per field from age against its own clock, weighted by consequence. Regulatory and ownership decay hurts more than imagery decay. Roll up to a record score.

Output confidence model
STEP 04

Surface

Show it in the field. Degrade loudly, not silently: an unverified difficulty rating should look different from a verified one. Absence of data is a state worth designing.

Output record UX
STEP 05

Target

Rank every trail by traffic × confidence loss. Hand the Trail Guide program a work queue instead of a coverage map. Contributors go where staleness actually costs a customer something.

Beyond Offroad

Most of this isn't only an Offroad problem. Hunt, Backcountry and Fish render blended content on independently moving clocks too, and onX is already sharing layers across all four. What the apps don't share is the machinery — each arrived at its own answer to the same question: how do you make content authoritative?

What's shared across all four

  • One ownership database. onX says Backcountry's land boundaries are built on the same database behind Hunt — 161M+ private properties from 3,100+ counties. Fish and Offroad draw on it too.
  • One basemap stack. Topo, satellite, hybrid, 3D and Recent Imagery appear across the suite.
  • One environmental layer set. Wildfire, air quality, weather, slope angle and aspect show up in more than one app.
  • One tool set. Waypoints, Tracker, Route Builder, Offline Maps, CarPlay — the same primitives everywhere.
  • One tier structure. Free / Premium / Elite, with private land ownership consistently gated to the top tier.
  • Offline-first everywhere. Every app assumes the customer loses signal at the moment of use.

Core content bet
A commissioned network — Trail Guides producing Guided Trails to a defined submission bar, with a four-tier rating system.
How it's acquired
Agency and MVUM sourcing reconciled in-house, then enriched by paid contributors in the field.
Distinctive
The only vertical that compensates contributors to produce content. Also the only one whose core question is legal permission rather than capability or opportunity.

Backcountry

Acquired & licensed authorship
Core content bet
770K+ trails and 30,000+ guidebook adventures — bought rather than built.
How it's acquired
Powered by Hiking Project, Mountain Project and Outdoor Project. Partnerships with Beacon Guidebooks and Colorado Mountain Club.
Distinctive
ATES — a formal terrain classification with a published regional coverage roadmap. Air quality cites AirNow.gov and states it updates hourly. It also has Trail Reports.

Fish

Institutional science
Core content bet
State fisheries survey data — CPUE, stocking history, species abundance and size distribution per lake.
How it's acquired
Agency datasets plus live stream gages. TroutRoutes brings 50,000+ streams and 280,000+ hand-curated access points.
Distinctive
The only vertical built on recurring scientific survey data with a natural cadence. Also runs its own quality classification for trout water.

The pattern worth naming: the data is shared, but the content mechanisms aren't. Four verticals independently built four ways of making content trustworthy — licensing at scale, paying contributors, acquiring communities, ingesting survey science. Each is strong in its own category. Very little of the machinery appears to move between them.

The clearest example: Hunt has already solved a structurally identical problem to MVUM. Hunting regulations are per-jurisdiction, republished annually, legally consequential, and inconsistent between states — and Hunt manages 15,880 units and 421 overlays against them. Offroad's regulatory challenge isn't unprecedented inside this company. It's been solved next door, for a different body of law.

Already happening

Layers cross verticals today

Avalanche Forecast, Slope Angle and Slope Aspect appear in the Offroad layer list — those are Backcountry-native. Private Land is Hunt's and it's in every app. Trail Reports run in both Offroad and Backcountry. The sharing is real and shipping.

ImplicationA confidence model built for one vertical could be inherited by all four, because the layers already are. Worth asking whether Trail Reports is one system or two.
Already being built

There's an open role for exactly this

onX is hiring a Platform PM to own how customer-generated data is collected, structured, and activated across all four verticals. Verification state is the most reusable thing that could sit in that layer.

ImplicationContent confidence should be a platform primitive, not an Offroad feature. Worth deciding early which team owns it.
Opportunity

One drive verifies a road for four apps

A hunter driving a forest road to a trailhead, a skier reaching a snowpark, an off-roader running a spur — they're on the same segment. Proof of passage collected in any app is evidence for every app.

ImplicationVerification density stops being an Offroad-only problem. The user base that matters is all of onX, not one vertical.
Opportunity

Access data is shared, so closures should be too

A gate closure or a seasonal restriction on a forest road affects a hunter, an off-roader, and a backcountry skier identically. Today that regulatory read has to be interpreted per vertical.

ImplicationOne agency relationship, negotiated once, serving four products — which changes the ROI case for the licensing work.
Opportunity

Contributor programs could share a queue

Trail Guides are Offroad's; Backcountry licenses guidebook authors; Hunt licenses research. The mechanism — rank by traffic × confidence loss, hand contributors a work queue — doesn't care which of those produced the content.

ImplicationThe program design is the reusable asset, not the contributor roster.
Open question

Where should this live?

If confidence is a platform capability, an Offroad content PM defines the requirements but doesn't own the pipe. That's a real organisational question and I'd rather ask it than assume an answer.

To askHow do Offroad and the Platform team currently split ownership of shared content?

The version of this role I'd want to do isn't just Offroad's content strategy. It's proving the model in the vertical with the hardest constraints — offline, regulated, legally consequential — and handing the platform team something already validated where it's most difficult.

What share of the network has a condition report from the current season — and is that tracked?
Where does MVUM sit in the pipeline, and what breaks when a forest republishes?
Guided Trails vs. inventory geometry — coverage gap, or deliberate scope?
What does content report against today: engagement, conversion, or retention?
